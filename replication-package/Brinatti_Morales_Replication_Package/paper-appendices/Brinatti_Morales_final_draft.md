# Firm Heterogeneity and the Impact of Immigration: Evidence from German Establishments<sup>∗</sup> 

### Agostina Brinatti<sup>†</sup> Nicolas Morales<sup>‡</sup> 

### June 2026

##### **Abstract** 

We document substantial heterogeneity in immigrant shares across employers in Germany, with large firms spending a higher share of their wage bill on immigrants than small firms. We provide evidence that fixed costs of hiring immigrants, partly policy-induced, are a key driver of this heterogeneity. We develop a model in which firms with heterogeneous productivities choose their immigrant share subject to fixed hiring costs, and show analytically how this heterogeneity affects the welfare gains from immigration. Two mechanisms are at play when firms differ in their immigrant shares. First, immigration induces native worker reallocation across firms, which affects the competition between immigrants and natives in the labor market. Second, immigration leads to larger declines in aggregate prices because firms’ marginal costs are more elastic to immigrant wages, and these cost reductions are concentrated among the most productive firms. Quantitatively, ignoring within-sector heterogeneity in immigrant shares understates native welfare gains from immigration by approximately 50%. Moreover, international trade dampens these gains, with 60% of the dampening effect explained by firm heterogeneity.

## **1 Introduction** 

Over the past two decades, the immigrant population in advanced economies has expanded by more than 80 percent, intensifying both academic and public debate over the consequences of immigration for native-born workers. A large body of research has studied this question, often implicitly assuming the existence of a representative firm. Yet firms differ along several dimensions such as size, export orientation, and workforce composition. 

Using a rich dataset from Germany, we document a new dimension of firm heterogeneity: large employers are more immigrant-intensive than small employers. To the extent that immigration inflows reallocate resources across firms, differences in firms’ immigrant shares may affect equilibrium adjustments in goods and labor markets. This paper asks whether and how heterogeneity in firms’ immigrant shares affects our understanding of the effects of immigration on the welfare of native-born workers. 

We characterize the relationship between employer size and immigrant intensity using establishment-level data from Germany between 2003 and 2011. We find a systematic relationship between employer size and immigrant intensity: the median establishment with more than 500 employees spends 5.6 percent of its wage bill on immigrants, whereas the median establishment with fewer than 10 employees spends zero. We also find evidence that fixed costs associated with hiring immigrant workers may drive this pattern. We exploit Germany’s 2011 opening of its labor market to EU New Member States (NMS) as a natural experiment that lowered hiring costs of these immigrants. Using an eventstudy approach, we show that small establishments expanded their hiring of NMS workers more than large establishments, relative to comparable workers from non-EU countries, consistent with the presence of fixed costs in hiring migrants prior to 2011.<sup>1</sup> 

Based on this evidence, we develop a model in which firms differ in their primitive productivity and their (endogenous) immigrant share to study the general equilibrium adjust-

Each good is produced by a firm that can employ immigrant and native workers, who are imperfect substitutes. We model the immigrant hiring decision by closely following the intermediate input sourcing literature (Blaum et al., 2018). Firms may choose to hire immigrant labor, but doing so requires paying a fixed cost for each additional source country from which they recruit. Consequently, larger firms find it profitable to recruit immigrants from more countries and to spend a larger share of their wage bill on immigrants. To fully capture the observed relationship between firm size and immigrant share, the model allows for firm-specific fixed costs of hiring immigrants, which are drawn from a joint distribution with innate productivity. 

We use a simplified model to show analytically that the welfare predictions of a model that ignores the relationship between firm size and immigrant share are biased. We do so by comparing welfare gains in a model with and without heterogeneity in immigrant intensities (but with heterogeneity in productivity). First, the competition between immigrants and natives in the labor market depends on the aggregate elasticity of substitution. With homogeneous immigrant shares across firms, the aggregate elasticity of substitution coincides with the within-firm elasticity. With heterogeneous firms, however, the aggregate elasticity also reflects substitution across firms (or goods), which governs changes in firms’ scale of production. When the (within-firm) substitution effect is stronger than the scale effect, immigrants crowd out natives at immigrant-intensive firms, who are reallocated toward native-intensive firms. This reallocation allows natives and immigrants to specialize in producing different goods, making the two groups less substitutable in the labor market. This, in turn, mitigates the wage pressure from immigration relative to the homogeneous case. Second, immigration generates a larger decline in aggregate prices because firms’ marginal costs are more elastic with respect to immigrant wages, and these cost reductions are disproportionately concentrated among the most productive firms. Overall, the _sign of the bias_ depends on whether the elasticity of substitution between immigrants and natives is larger or smaller than the elasticity of demand, while

the _magnitude of the bias_ depends on the joint distribution of firm productivity and immigrant-hiring costs. 

We also show analytically that immigration gains in an open economy can be larger or lower than in a closed economy due to firm heterogeneity in immigrant share. In an open economy, the larger market size amplifies the scale effect at immigrant-intensive firms, reducing the displacement of native coworkers and weakening reallocation toward nativeintensive firms. At the same time, the accompanying increase in profits induces firms to pay the fixed cost to source immigrants from more countries, increasing substitution away from natives and thus reinforcing reallocation toward native-intensive firms. 

We estimate the elasticity of demand from the average firms’ markups, following Oberfield and Raval (2014). The (within-firm) substitution between immigrants and natives is structurally estimated using the firm’s first-order condition with respect to immigrant and native labor. We regress the firm-level relative wage bill between immigrants and natives on relative employment. We extend Ottaviano and Peri (2012)’s approach to allow for heterogeneity in workers’ abilities, as suggested by our model, and using a model-based firm-level instrument. At a 1% confidence level, our estimates suggest that the elasticity of substitution between immigrants and natives within firms exceeds the elasticity of demand. Thus, the observed heterogeneity in immigrant share is expected to generate larger welfare gains from immigration. We calibrate other parameters jointly to match key targeted micro- and macro-level moments in Germany between 2003 and 2011. Importantly, the observed relationship of immigrant share and firm size, our novel fact, disciplines the parameters of the joint distribution of productivities and fixed costs. 

We verify that the calibrated model properly captures the mechanisms through which firm heterogeneity in immigrant share affects welfare. Our analytical results show that the heterogeneity in immigrant shares leads to heterogeneity in the elasticity of sales and the elasticity of the immigrant-to-native wage bill ratio to immigrant labor inflows. Therefore, we compare the predicted changes in firms’ sales and the immigrant-to-native wage bill ratio due to an exogenous immigrant inflow between the model and the data.

To that end, we regress firm revenues and the relative wage bill ratio on the share of immigrants in the local labor market and its interaction with firm size. We use these reduced-form estimates to compute the effect of a 1% increase in the share of immigrants in the labor market for firms in different size deciles and find that the model does a good job of matching its data counterparts. 

We use the calibrated model to measure the real wage (or welfare) effects of a 20% increase in the total number of immigrants, motivated by the inflow experienced by Germany between 2011 and 2017. We find that native workers in both sectors benefit from immigration despite the increased competition in the labor market, as the cost of the consumption basket falls relative to their wages. Production increases for both sectors, but more so in the tradable sector, where firms are more intensive in immigrant labor. Firms in the top size decile of the tradable sector, being the most immigrant-intensive, absorb a large share of incoming immigrants, leading to the reallocation of many native workers to other firms. Given their size, large firms in the non-tradable sector absorb a significant portion of these native workers. This reallocation between firms explains most of the cross-sector reallocation of native workers. Natives also reallocate within sectors toward less immigrant-intensive firms. Overall, welfare gains of native-born workers and firm owners are 0.11% and 1.31%, respectively. In monetary terms, these gains amount to $1.8 billion for native workers and $20.8 billion for firm owners.<sup>2</sup> 

We find that the quantitative relevance of both analytical results is large in the aggregate. First, the version of our model that removes this within-industry heterogeneity in immigrant shares, while still allowing immigrant shares to vary across industries and Hicks-neutral productivity to vary across firms, predicts welfare gains roughly 50% lower. Second, in a counterfactual trade-autarky economy, welfare gains from immigration are approximately 10% larger. Of this trade-dampening effect, 60% is accounted for by within-sector heterogeneity in firms’ migrant shares.<sup>3</sup>

## **2 Data** 

We use a detailed, employer-employee matched dataset from Germany provided by the Institute for Employment Research (IAB). The main data source is the Longitudinal Establishment Panel (LIAB), which includes records for a large sample of establishments over the period 2003-2011 (Heining et al., 2016).<sup>7</sup> The dataset contains full employment trajectories for each employee who worked at least one day for one of the establishments in the sample during the period. It also includes employee information on citizenship, occupation, education, and daily wage. While our ideal measure for immigration status would be country of birth, the data reports citizenship, where countries are grouped into ten regions. We classify as immigrants those who report non-German citizenship in at least two periods. Appendix A.1 discusses the details and robustness of this definition. 

On the establishment side, the dataset contains information on industry, location, and financials such as revenues, material use, and export share, among others (Institut f¨ur Arbeitsmarkt- und Berufsforschung (IAB), 2013). It is important to note that the data is at the establishment level, and it is not possible to link multiple establishments to a single firm. Throughout the paper, we use establishment and firm interchangeably.<sup>8</sup> Also, we apply survey weights to get representative aggregates whenever necessary.

## **3 Firm Heterogeneity in Immigrant Share** 

We begin our analysis by establishing a key fact: larger establishments are more intensive on immigrant labor than smaller establishments. To assess the implications of this heterogeneity for the welfare effects of immigration on native workers, we must understand the microfoundations that determine immigrant employment shares. To do so, we begin by discarding natural potential explanations that are inconsistent with salient features of the data. Second, we show that fixed costs associated with hiring immigrant workers likely constitute the primary explanatory factor of the observed systematic relationship between employer size and immigrant intensity.<sup>10</sup>

### **3.3 Fixed costs to hire immigrants** 

#### **3.3.1 Institutional aspects and descriptive evidence of fixed costs** 

During our period of analysis, most non-EU nationals were allowed to migrate to Germany only after securing a job offer. This arrangement, similar to the U.S. system, places the burden of navigating administrative procedures, as well as identifying and screening candidates, on the sponsoring employer (see Appendix Section A.3). 

In addition to these policy-induced fixed costs, firms may incur costs required to build a recruitment channel from specific source countries. For example, firms may need to develop internal capacity to evaluate foreign credentials or learn how to access networks of suitable workers from a given origin country. This is consistent with evidence from the OECD and the German Chamber of Commerce and Industry (DIHK), who ran an employer survey in 2010 asking for the reasons why firms with unfilled vacancies did not attempt to hire workers from abroad (OECD, 2013). The top three reasons for this trend are the lack of German language skills of candidates, unclear and complex administrative procedures, and difficulties contacting candidates abroad. 

The survey also shows that these barriers disproportionately impact small and medium enterprises (SMEs). One reason is that SMEs have fewer resources than large firms to overcome these barriers. For instance, larger firms are better positioned to afford specialized HR services that navigate the complexities of the immigration system and facilitate contacting candidates abroad. Moreover, the German Employment Agency verifies that the employer’s petition is legitimate and that the working conditions offered to the foreign worker are not below those offered to German employees in the same occupation. According to the report, these checks tend to be more severe when the 

9 

employer is not well-known, as tends to be the case for SMEs. The difficulty of SMEs to hire immigrants has even been the subject of public policy debate. 

We complement this anecdotal evidence with direct evidence from the data, including the substantial share of establishments that do not hire immigrants and the lumpiness of the hiring process (see Appendix Section A.5). 

#### **3.3.2 Causal evidence of fixed costs to hire immigrants before 2011** 

This anecdotal and cross-sectional evidence is suggestive of fixed costs, but it does not isolate their effect from other unobserved factors that may influence immigrant hiring decisions across employers of different sizes. We make progress in this direction by using the 2011 incorporation of the New Member States (NMS) into the EU, which granted citizens from 10 countries unrestricted access to the German labor market.<sup>12</sup> This policy change provides a natural experiment that lowered fixed and variable hiring costs for NMS immigrants relative to non-EU nationals, who continued to face the same restrictions. Consistent with the existence of fixed costs to hire these immigrants before 2011, we find that smaller establishments exhibited a stronger increase in NMS hiring than larger establishments.

### **3.4 Additional evidence guiding modeling assumptions** 

For employers to optimally hire both immigrants and natives despite fixed costs, such workers must differ in the workplace. If they were perfect substitutes, firms would hire either only native-born or immigrant-born workers. Appendix A.6 shows that immigrants — from different countries have different bundles of characteristics such as age, gender, — and education and specialize in different occupations than natives. We also provide evidence consistent with this specialization being related to origin-specific comparative advantage. Based on this evidence, our model will treat immigrants and natives, as well as immigrants from different origin countries, as imperfect substitutes in production. 

## **4 The Model** 

This section studies how firm heterogeneity in immigrant share shapes the economy’s adjustment to immigration inflows and the effects on native workers’ welfare. We develop a model informed by the evidence from previous sections. Firms with heterogeneous productivities seek to hire immigrant labor because immigrants are imperfect substitutes for native workers. However, doing so requires two types of fixed costs: an initial cost to begin hiring immigrants and an additional cost for each new country they source immigrants from. This cost structure implies that larger, more productive firms spend a larger share of their wage bill on immigrants (Figure 1), are more likely to hire immigrants (Figure A3), and more likely to recruit immigrants from more countries (Figure A6a) compared to smaller firms. We use the model to study the role of firm heterogeneity in immigrant share in the effects of an immigrant labor supply shock. How do firms choose their optimal native share? They face a trade-off between the drop in the marginal cost of production induced by the complementarity of hiring from an additional country and the fixed cost to source from that additional country. While the _levels_ of the responses between the data and the model are not directly comparable, the _relative_ differences across deciles are. The levels are not comparable because the estimates of the empirical regression identify relative effects, but might not identify the level effect of the shock (see Nakamura and Steinsson (2014); Wolf (2023) for a discussion on the “The Missing Intercept Problem”). To compare the relative differences, we normalize both the data and model responses to be zero in the first decile and rescale the normalized values as percentage changes with respect to their own average.<sup>33</sup> Figure 3 shows that, in both sectors, the model-predicted changes closely match the revenue and relative immigrantto-native wage-bill responses observed in the data. We therefore interpret the model’s performance as successful in matching these untargeted moments, which are crucial for the welfare effect of immigration.<sup>34</sup>

### **6.4 Comparing policies that affect firm heterogeneity** 

Beyond policies that affect the inflow of immigrants into the country, which was studied in prior literature, our model allows us to also study policies that alter the hiring costs of immigrants for specific firms. Appendix Section D.2 studies alternative policies that reduce immigrant hiring costs for a subset of firms. Policies targeting smaller firms mainly benefit non-tradable sector firms because they face higher hiring frictions.<sup>42</sup> Small firms in the tradable sector are not similarly affected, as the cost reduction does not offset productivity differences with large competitors in the sector. The effects on native workers are positive but small. We find that lowering fixed costs for high-productivity firms in the tradable sector has the largest welfare effects for natives as firms affected face a more elastic demand, and can expand production without crowding out as many natives. 

## **7 Conclusion** 

This paper documents substantial heterogeneity in immigrant shares across employers, with larger firms employing immigrants more intensively than smaller firms due to fixed costs of hiring immigrants. 

Motivated by these facts, we set up a model to study analytically the welfare implication of firm heterogeneity in immigrant shares for the welfare effects of immigration. First, we

Second, we study the implications of firm heterogeneity in immigrant shares for the characteristics of countries that make immigrant flows more valuable. In particular, we show that, due to migrant-share heterogeneity, international trade can either amplify or dampen the welfare gains from immigration. 

We calibrate the model to match our novel evidence and use it to assess the quantitative relevance of these theoretical results. We find that a 20% increase in the number of immigrants raises welfare by 0.11% for native-born workers and by 1.31% for firm owners, corresponding to gains of $1.8 billion and $20.8 billion, respectively. Regarding the first analytical result, we find that a model without within-industry heterogeneity in immigrant shares predicts that the welfare gains for native workers would be approximately 55% lower. This means that immigration induces a reallocation of resources across firms with quantitatively important aggregate implications. Thus, ignoring the documented immigrant share heterogeneity may significantly underestimate the role of international trade in mitigating the gains from immigration.

### **A.1 Data and Summary Statistics** 

Our data separates workers into 10 nationality groups: 1) Germany, 2) Europe highincome: France, United Kingdom, Netherlands, Belgium, Austria, Switzerland, Finland, and Sweden, 3) EU middle-income: Italy, Spain, Greece, and Portugal, 4) New member states: countries that joined the EU after 2004, 5) countries of former Yugoslavia not in the EU, 6) Turkey, 7) all other European countries including Russia, 8) Asia-Pacific, 9) Africa and Middle East, and 10) the Americas. 

A key variable needed for our analysis is workers’ immigration status at a given establishment, but the German social security data records citizenship as opposed to country of birth. Since we are interested in country of birth, we redefine this key variable to make sure we count immigrants properly. The most common recoding is when observing individuals with foreign citizenship who become Germans in the next period. ### **A.2 Additional Facts on Heterogeneity Across Firm Size** 

We show that our baseline fact cannot solely be explained by large firms having different skill intensities. For example, Peri and Sparber (2009) find that long-term immigrants (over 10 years in the country) perform tasks more similar to natives than recent immigrants, though differences with natives do not fully disappear. There is also persistence in employment, where immigrants might find jobs at big firms when arriving in the country, and then stay at those firms as their career progresses.

These differences with respect to equations 13 and 14 have relevant aggregate implications. First, in the home-bias model, immigration reduces the immigrant market wage and, all else equal, the marginal cost of production drops proportionally to the share of immigrants in the cost of production. This elasticity of marginal cost of production is stronger in the model with fixed costs because the drop in costs increases profits, and so the firm can pay more times the fixed cost to source immigrants from more origins, which further reduces the unit cost of production. ### **C.3 Tests of the Identification Strategy** 

Our instrument exploits differential exposure of firms to immigrant arrivals from different countries, where the firm exposure is based on the nationality composition of their workforce. Thus, our setting relies on an identifying assumption in terms of the shares Goldsmith-Pinkham et al. (2020). This section describes the variation underlying our instrument and tests the identifying assumption. 

**Shift-share Instrument Diagnostics:** We describe the variation that the instrument uses by calculating the Rottemberg weights of the Bartik instrument. To do this, we write the first stage coefficient on the shift-share instrument as a combination of the estimates of nine separate first stage regressions. Each of these “just identified” regressions uses an instrument that is constructed with the initial share and shock of only one of our nine origin regions. The weights in which each of these nine instruments affects the overall instrument are called Rottemberg weights, which we calculate using the code provided by Goldsmith-Pinkham et al. (2020) and denote as _α_ . Each origin region is affected each year by a national-level shock, which we denote by _G_ . The just-identified coefficients are denoted by _β_ . 

As shown in Table C2, panel A, all the Rottemberg weights are positive, meaning that our regression is likely not subject to misspecification and our coefficient is likely to have a local average treatment effect-like interpretation. Panel B shows the correlation between the weights, the shocks, and the just-identified coefficients. Panel C shows the top five origin regions in terms of the Rottemberg weights, which indicates they are likely to play an important role in the variation of our instrument. The country with the largest weight is middle-income EU countries (0.175), followed by New EU countries (0.143), other European countries not in the EU (0.143), high-income EU countries (0.136), and Turkey (0.125). It is reassuring that no single region accounts for a large majority of the variation in our instrument.

### **C.4 Estimation of Remaining Structural Parameters** 

**Elasticity of labor supply** _ν_ **:** We estimate _ν_ using information on the variability of wages in our sample. A property of the Fr´echet distribution is that it governs both, the dispersion in abilities and the elasticity of labor supply. If abilities across sectors and countries are very dispersed, individuals will not respond as much in their choices when 

75 

wages change. On the other hand, if ability draws across options are very concentrated, workers’ choices will be very sensitive to changes in the wage. 

To estimate _ν_ , we follow the literature estimating the labor supply elasticity using properties of extreme value distributions and data of the distribution of wages (e.g., Hsieh et al. (2019), Fan (2019), and Lee (2020)). Specifically, the Fr´echet distribution assumption implies that the wage distribution for workers from the same nationality who choose the same sector-country pair has a squared coefficient of variation as in equation 49. 



Following this relationship, we can compute the left-hand side from the data to recover the implied value of _ν_ . Note that the elasticity governs both the sector choice for natives and migrants, and the country choice for migrants. Since we only have data on workers living in Germany, our estimation predominantly captures the elasticity of supply across sectors.<sup>57</sup> 

To estimate _ν_ with our data, we restrict the sample to German individuals between 25 and 65 years of age who work at the establishments in our sample between 2003 and 2011.<sup>58</sup> We run an individual-level regression of log wages on different sets of fixed effects and take the residual of that regression. We consider three sets of fixed effects: 1) industry-year fixed effects, 2) industry-year fixed effects, district fixed effects, and age and gender controls, and 3) a fully saturated model that includes age-gender-district-industryyear fixed effects. Implicitly, the residual of these regressions is the data counterpart of the ability draws taken by individuals in the model. We then take the exponent of that residual and compute the left-hand side of equation 49. Table C6 shows that our estimates ˆ range from 4.90 to 6.14. ### **D.2 Comparing Policies that Affect Firm Heterogeneity** 

This section studies policies that alter the hiring costs of immigrants for specific firms. In Section 3.3, we mentioned that the German government implemented policies to reduce the frictions faced by small and medium enterprises (SMEs) for recruiting immigrants. 

82 

These policies motivate our first counterfactual analysis, referred to as “Policy 1”. We divide firms into two groups based on whether their productivity _ψj_ is above or below the median. The policy lowers the immigrant recruitment cost _fj_ for below-median productivity firms to the 10th percentile of the _fj_ distribution. We compare it with three alternative policies to shed light on how policies that alter the distribution of firms’ immigrant intensities can affect aggregate outcomes. An alternative policy reduces frictions by the same total amount but for firms in the top half of the productivity distribution (“Policy 2”). This policy aligns with efforts to facilitate hiring by large firms, such as the U.S. L-1 visa program, which allows multinational companies to transfer employees across subsidiaries and streamline applications for multiple workers in the same role. While Policies 1 and 2 are not sector-specific, they primarily affect nontradable firms because they face higher frictions for hiring immigrants. This motivates two additional policies: reducing frictions in the tradable sector by the same total amount, either for firms below the sector’s median productivity (“Policy 3”) or for those above it (“Policy 4”). 

We begin by studying Policy 1’s effect on aggregate outcomes (Column 1 of Table D2). Appendix Figure D1 plots the reallocation of immigrants and native workers across sectors and productivity deciles. The policy substantially raises immigrant employment in below-median productivity firms within the non-tradable sector, while the increase in the tradable sector is modest. This difference across sectors arises for two main reasons. First, non-tradable firms face higher initial costs _fj_ than firms in the tradable sector, so reducing these costs to the 10th percentile represents a larger relative shock. Second, low-productivity firms in the tradable sector hold a much smaller market share within their industry.<sup>61</sup> As a result, even with lower fixed costs, unproductive firms in the tradable sector remain at a steep disadvantage relative to their domestic competitors and see modest expansion in hiring or production. The increase in immigrant hiring by small firms in the non-tradable sector crowds out native coworkers, who relocate to higher productivity firms in both sectors. Regarding the aggregate effects of the policy, real earnings for native workers increase but only slightly due to countervailing effects. On one hand, immigrant employment increases, which pushes prices down for all consumers. On the other hand, the influx of immigrants reduces native-born wages due to higher labor market competition in the non-tradable sector. Natives mitigate such losses by moving to the tradable sector, but overall earnings decrease. As a result, real earnings change only slightly. 

We now turn to the analysis of alternative policies, highlighting how aggregate outcomes differ relative to Policy 1. Lowering _fj_ for firms at the top of the productivity distribution
